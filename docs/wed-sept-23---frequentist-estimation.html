8 Wed Sept 23 - Frequentist estimation

Estimating population parameters from sample data is the goal of statistics. A good estimator should be unbiased.

An example of a population parameter is the mean or the standard deviation. You likely already know the formula to calculate the mean, \(\bar{y}\), from a sample of size, \(n\). The formula to calculate the standard deviation is,

\[ s = \sqrt{\dfrac{\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \bar{y})}{n-1}}. \]

Table 2.1 in Quinn and Keough lists other commonly estimated parameters, statistics, and their formulas.

Degrees for freedom are the number of observations that are “free to vary” when we estimate a parameter. For example, for a sample of \(n\) observations we have \(n-1\) degrees of freedom to estimate the standard deviation because we use one degree of freedom to estimate the mean. Often the degrees of freedom are \(n-p\) where \(p\) is the number of parameters already estimated.

Interval estimates provide a range of values that might include the parameter with a given probability. The confidence interval is a range which we have some confidence that the true value of the parameter might lie in.

8.1 Methods of estimation (frequentist estimation)

Ordinary least squares (OLS). The OLS estimator for a parameter minimizes the sum of the squared differences between each sample estimate adn the estimate of the population mean, \(\mu\). Assumes a normal distribution for confidence intervals.

Maximum likelihood (ML). The ML estimator for a parameter is the one that maximizes the likelihood of observing the data.

8.2 Simple parameters and statistics

Given a sample of observations \((y_1, y_2, \dots, y_n)\) we would usually like to estimate some measures of the data’s central tendency and its spread. The center of the distribution can be measured by the mean or median. The spread (or variability) can be measured by the standard deviation, or the variance, \(s^2\), where the sample variance is calculated as,

\[ s^2 = \dfrac{\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - \bar{y})}{n-1}, \]

and where the numerator is the residual sum of squares (RSS), and the denomenator is \(n-1\) (rather than \(n\)) because the \(n-1\) is needed for \(s^2\) to be an unbiased estimate of the population variance. The units of the variance are squared that of the original measurements, while for the standard deviation, \(s = \sqrt{s^2}\), are same units as the original measurements.

8.3 Sampling the distribution mean

Having a point estimate of the population mean is only the first step under frequentist estimation, we also need to estimate how variable our estimate might be under repeated sampling from the population.

Assume that our sample, \((y_1, y_2, \dots, y_n)\), has come from a normally distributed population. We usually only take one sample from a population, but if we took many samples and plotted the distribution of the sample means the sampling distribution of means ahs the following properties:

  • The probability distribution of means of samples from normal distributions are also normally distributed.
  • As the sample size, \(n\), increases, the probability distributions of means of samples from any distribution will approach a normal distribution. This is a result of the central limit theorem.
  • The expected value, or the mean, of the probability distribution of the sample means is the mean of the population (\(\mu\)) from which the samples where taken.

8.4 Standard error of the sample mean

Let’s consider that the sample means have a normal distribution. We can calculate their variance, just as we calculated their mean values. The expected value of the standard deviation (SD) of the sample mean is,

\[ \sigma_{\bar{y}} = \frac{\sigma}{n}, \]

where \(\sigma\) is the SD of the original population from which the repeated samples were taken. We rarely have many samples from the same population. The SD of the sample means is called the standard error or the mean, and is estimated as,

\[ s_{\bar{y}} = \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}, \]

where \(s\) is the sample estimate of the SD original population (the population the samples are drawn from) adn \(n\) is the sample size. The standard error describes the error in estimating the population mean, \(\mu\), using the sample estimator, \(\bar{y}\). If the standard error, \(s_{\bar{y}}\) is large, then repeated sampling and estimation of the population mean would generate highly variable estimates.

8.5 Confidence estimates for the population mean

We can convert any sample mean into its equivalent value from a standard normal distribution as,

\[ z = \frac{\bar{y}-\mu}{\sigma_{\bar{y}}}, \]

where \(\sigma_{\bar{y}}\) is the standard error (i.e., the SD of the mean). We can then use the quantiles of the standard normal distribution to estimate the range of values that the population mean occurs within withn a given probability, for example:

\[ P(\bar{y}-1.96\sigma_{\bar{y}} \leq \mu \leq \bar{y}+1.96\sigma_{\bar{y}}) = 0.95, \]

which is an interval estimate for the population mean, \(\mu\), and specifically, given the values above means that there is a 95\(\%\) chance that the sample mean is within the interval, \([\bar{y}-1.96\sigma_{\bar{y}},\bar{y}+1.96\sigma_{\bar{y}}]\).

A problem with the fomula above is that we do not know \(\sigma_{\bar{y}}\) which is the population variance, i.e., the true variance, of the distribution of sample means. Generally, we can never know these true population values, we can only try to estimate the true values using estimators.

A version of the formula for a 95\(\%\) confidence interval for the population mean, \(\mu\), where we can evaluate all the quantities in the formula is,

\[ \bar{y} - t_{0.025, n-1}\dfrac{s}{\sqrt{n}} \quad \leq \quad \mu \quad \leq \quad \bar{y} + t_{0.025, n-1}\dfrac{s}{\sqrt{n}}. \]

The sample mean is \(\bar{y} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n y_i\) for a sample with \(n\) observations and where \(s\) is the sample standard deviation (see earlier in these notes for the formula), and the t-statistic (which approaches the standard normal distribution as \(n \to \infty\)) for the quantiles 0.025 and 0.975 with \(n-1\) degrees of freedom can be determined from a look-up table or with the code:

n = 100 # number of observations in sample
qt(0.025, n-1)
## [1] -1.984217

In the code above \(n\) is specifically set to 100. There is a 95\(\%\) probability that the population mean is between these values. Practice calculating the 95\(\%\) confidence interval for some data. Understand what the estimated quantity means.

8.5.1 Interpretation of confidence intervals for the population mean.

We usually do not consider the population mean, \(\mu\), to be a random variable. Rather, we understand the population mean to be fixed value, although we do not know what this value is. The confidence interval is a probability statement about the interval rather than the parameter itself.

8.6 Standard errors and confidence intervals for other statistics

The standard error (SE) is the standard deviation (SD) of the probability distribution of a specific statistic, so we can calculate the SE for statistics other than the mean (as described in Sokal and Rohlf, 2012).

However, the sample variance is known to follow a \(\chi^2\) distribution and so a 95\(\%\) confidence interval for the population variance is calculated as: \[ \left[\dfrac{s^2(n-1)}{\chi^2_{0.025, n-1}}, \dfrac{s^2(n-1)}{\chi^2_{0.975, n-1}}\right], \]

where \(\chi_{\alpha/2, \text{df}}\) is the \(\chi^2\) distribution evaluated at the quantile, \(\alpha/2\) with df degrees of freedom. This value needs to be looked up and can be evaluate in R using the qchisq() function. See the help file here for more details.

8.7 Resampling methods for frequentist estimation

The methods for estimating confidence intervals in this section rely on assumptions that the statistic’s sampling distribution follows a normal or t-distribution. We may be not be able to justify this assumption in some cases, or it might be difficult to derive an unbiased sample estimator.

Without any other information, our best guess as to the distribution of the population is the observations in our sample.

8.7.1 Bootstrap

A bootstrap estimator is determined by randomly by resampling the data with replacement to create many new hypothetical data sets each with the same sample size. We calculate our statistic of interest for each of the many resampled (with replacement) hypothetical data sets. The bootstrap estimate of the parameter is the mean of these statistics. The SE of the bootstrap estimate is their SD.

Confidence intervals for bootstrap methods are: percentile method, the bias corrected method, and the accelerated bootstrap (see Efron and Hastie 2016). Efron and Hastie recommended at least 2,000 samples for confidence interval estimation using bootstrap methods.

The percentile method involves ranking the estimated statistic values for each sample. If there are 1000 samples, the 95\(\%\) confidence interval is estimated as [25th ranked estimate, 975th estimate].

Example of resampled (with replacement) data sets for the pea length data:

pea.length = t(pea.length) # sample() function only works when pea.length is a row vector.

8.7.2 Jackknife

The jackknife is an earlier alternative to bootstrapping that is less computer intensive. This approach involves recalculating statistics after removing successive data points.
